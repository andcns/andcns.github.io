Technical art · Runtime style system

Project Winterlight

A controlled FPS prototype built around a runtime transition between modern 3D and 1990s-inspired presentation while the gameplay state stays consistent.

RoleSolo developer / technical artist
ToolsUnity 6 / URP · C# · Blender · Substance 3D Painter
Outcome74 · First-class dissertation · Digital Shark Expo nominee

01 · Technical art / System overview

One gameplay state. Two presentation systems.

I approached the problem from the art side first: the modern and retro versions needed to feel like two complete visual states without becoming two different games. A central style state coordinates the presentation layers, while the underlying gameplay stays consistent.

The transition is triggered by a scripted event or debug input. StyleSwapManager owns the Modern / Retro state and blocks overlapping swaps. Transition FX briefly pauses registered gameplay systems, plays the CRT roll / tear / settle sequence, commits the new state at one controlled midpoint, then broadcasts the presentation state through a GlobalStyleSwapEvent ScriptableObject event channel.

Listeners update the environment material sets, enemy 3D/billboard representation and damage VFX, player weapon presentation, HUD art/fonts/face states/crosshair, and audio mixer snapshots. The level geometry and underlying gameplay remain unchanged.

My role: I approached Winterlight from a 3D art and technical-art perspective: defining how the visual states should behave, authoring the key 3D assets and presentation, and then building the Unity setup needed to make those elements work together in real time. I configured, integrated, tested and debugged the system in-engine, while the C# implementation used AI assistance based on my requirements and system design.

03 · Research context / Constraint

The research gave the system a constraint.

Winterlight was built for my final-year dissertation. Modern, retro and transition conditions had to remain mechanically comparable, so the visual transformation could be tested without changing the game underneath it.

I built three controlled conditions — Modern, Retro and Transition — around the same level, combat rules and enemy behaviour. The experiment used the Immersive Experience Questionnaire (IEQ) with 12 participants, four per condition. Mean IEQ scores were 4.90 for Modern, 5.62 for Transition and 6.06 for Retro.

04 · Problems / Solutions

Keeping two enemies mechanically equivalent.

The modern enemy used skeletal 3D animation while the retro version used billboard sprites. Letting either animation directly own combat timing risked making the two visual conditions play differently.

Gameplay drives presentation

EnemyRangedBrain and RangedAttackModule own navigation and attack state. EnemyVisualAnimatorProxy sends the same parameters to both animators, while EnemyStyleSwapListener controls which renderer and damage VFX are presented.

For the system-wide transition, gameplay systems are briefly paused, the CRT/glitch sequence plays, the global state is committed once at the midpoint, and play resumes. This prevents environment, HUD, enemy and audio layers from visibly changing at different times.

After a style event, PlayerCombatController waits one frame before caching the style-appropriate shoot module, avoiding a stale module while weapon objects are still applying their new active state.

05 · Combat / Data-driven configuration

Four ammo behaviours, one weapon platform.

Bullet, shell, rocket and plasma configurations share the same player combat flow. Their tuning and audiovisual feedback live in authorable ScriptableObject data rather than four separate weapon implementations.

Each AmmoTypeConfig stores combat tuning and presentation data including damage, fire cooldown, modern VFX, retro sprite VFX, SFX, pitch, recoil and cooldown smoke. PlayerCombatController consumes the same configuration regardless of visual state, while the modern and retro shooting modules use style-appropriate presentation.

06 · Outcome / Reflection

The transition held together.

The project received a mark of 74 and was nominated for the Digital Shark Expo. The overall ANOVA condition effect was p = .017. The sample was small (n=12) and no post-hoc pairwise tests were conducted, so I treat the statistical result as indicative rather than definitive.

Winterlight was the first project where I properly shifted my mindset from visual artist to technical artist. The real-time constraint, combined with the academic research side of the project, forced me to think not just about making art, but about how to build systems around it and turn visual ideas into something the engine could control consistently.

By the end of the project I felt I had moved from thinking mainly as a visual artist to thinking much more like a systems designer. I also discovered how satisfying it is to systematise an abstract idea — breaking the problem apart and eventually seeing that idea materialise in real time.
